Dear Editor,

I am writing as the corresponding author to submit our proposal for a Comment tentatively titled “Demand flexibility must account for consumer capital costs” for consideration by Nature Reviews Clean Technology. I am an Associate Professor at Tsinghua University and an Associate Editor of IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, where I handle submissions on demand response and electricity markets. Through my research and editorial work, I have repeatedly encountered a gap between how flexibility is described in the literature and how industrial consumers make investment and operating decisions.

Demand flexibility is becoming increasingly important as renewable generation, industrial electrification, hydrogen production and other clean technologies reshape electricity supply and demand. Yet many large loads respond much less to electricity prices than their technical controllability would suggest. Recent IEA reports, including “The Value of Demand Flexibility” (2025) and “Scaling Up Demand Flexibility” (2026), emphasize inadequate incentives, the cost of enabling technologies, limited market access and a lack of consumer trust. These barriers matter, but another economic constraint is often absent from the discussion. Shifting electricity use can lower asset utilization, require additional productive capacity and tie up capital in inventories.

My coauthor, Ruike Lyu, recently completed his PhD on industrial demand flexibility at Tsinghua University and is now a Postdoctoral Research Scientist at Princeton University. His five years of doctoral research examined this gap directly from the perspective of industrial operations. Our recent Nature Energy article, “Industrial overcapacity can enable seasonal flexibility in electricity use,” found, counterintuitively, that overcapacity, usually regarded as inefficient, can make seasonal flexibility economically viable. The fact that this result seems surprising points to a gap in current thinking. Studies that start from technical controllability and electricity-cost savings can miss the role of asset utilization and consumer capital costs. Once these costs are considered, the value of existing overcapacity is easier to understand.

Ruike’s direct research experience and my work in these areas, together with our discussions of recent studies and practice, led us to believe that this overlooked issue deserves wider attention.

The proposed Comment will ask when shifting electricity use is economically worthwhile for a consumer. It will explain why technical controllability does not necessarily imply economic responsiveness and why the answer varies across technologies and timescales. Its recommendations will include assessing the full cost borne by consumers, starting with facilities that have idle or redundant capacity and low inventory-carrying costs, and using each flexible resource over suitable timescales and only where its system value justifies its cost.

The Comment will show how overlooking consumer capital costs can overstate the flexibility available from electrified industry, hydrogen production and other large loads. It will shift the question from how much demand is technically controllable to how much is economically usable, and under what conditions. We believe this distinction is timely for readers of Nature Reviews Clean Technology and hope the Comment will prompt further research and discussion about the realistic role of demand flexibility in clean-energy systems.

We have included a synopsis of the proposed Comment. If the topic is of interest, we would welcome any feedback from the editors.

Thank you for considering our proposal.
